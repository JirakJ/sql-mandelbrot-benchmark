Jalapeño vs Blackwell: what the 1.9× perf/watt actually measures

13 Sep 20265 min readJakub Jirák

OpenAI published the first numbers for its in-house inference chip on SemiAnalysis' InferenceX benchmark: 1.5–1.9× more tokens per kilowatt than Nvidia Blackwell, up to 3.6× lower latency. The numbers are real. What they measure is narrower than the headline — and most of the gap is already visible in the memory spec sheet.

The dataThe numbers, as published

Three models, three comparisons, one operating point each. Throughput is InferenceX's "mixed tokens per second per kilowatt"; latency is end-to-end for the request.

On GPT-OSS the time between tokens dropped from 1.87 ms to 0.69 ms. The part behind it: one reticle-sized compute die on TSMC N3P, 13.4 PFLOPS of MXFP4 on the B0 stepping, six HBM4 stacks for 216 GiB and 15.4 TB/s, a 700 W package TDP with a measured sustained draw of roughly 550 W. The Blackwell parts it was compared against are rated at 1 200 W (GB200) and 1 400 W (GB300) per GPU.

MethodologyWhat InferenceX actually counts

Before crediting the architecture, read the axis labels. Four things about the benchmark change how the table above should be interpreted.

  • The denominator is all-in utility power, not chip TDP. InferenceX divides output tokens by the megawatts the whole deployment pulls from the wall — accelerator rack, host rack, networking. Jalapeño's reference deployment is a two-rack pair at roughly 160 kW. A lean host rack is worth as much here as a good compute die.
  • It's a curve, not a point. The benchmark sweeps interactivity (tokens per second per user) against concurrency. "1.9×" is the ratio at one operating point on that curve, and the ratio changes along it.
  • Mixed tokens, short context. Prefill and decode tokens are combined in one figure, at 8k input / 1k output. The long, multi-turn agent traces that dominate real production traffic — InferenceX calls that suite AgentX — have not been published for Jalapeño yet.
  • Speculative decoding was off on Jalapeño while the Nvidia entries ran with multi-token prediction. That handicap runs against OpenAI's own chip, which makes the result more credible, not less.
ProvenanceThe numbers were produced by OpenAI. SemiAnalysis observed the runs in OpenAI's lab and reviewed them, but did not execute the full suite independently. The recipes — model, framework, precision, parallelism — are committed to the public InferenceX repository as shell scripts, so a third party with silicon could reproduce them. Nobody outside OpenAI has silicon.

RooflineBandwidth per watt explains the shape

Decoding is a memory-bandwidth problem. Every generated token streams the active weights and the KV cache through HBM once; the arithmetic per byte is tiny. So the first thing to normalize is not FLOPS but bytes per second per watt:

PartMemoryBandwidthPowerGB/s per W
Jalapeño6× HBM4, 216 GiB15.4 TB/s700 W22.0
GB200 (B200)HBM3e, 192 GB8 TB/s1 200 W6.7
GB300 (B300)HBM3e, 288 GB8 TB/s1 400 W5.7
RubinHBM4, 288 GB~22 TB/s900–1 150 W19–24

Against Blackwell, Jalapeño has a 3.3–3.9× advantage in bandwidth per watt before a single instruction executes. The measured advantage in tokens per kilowatt is 1.5–1.9×. Roughly half of the memory-system edge disappears into the parts of the workload that are not a weight stream — compute-bound prefill, host CPUs, network, cooling — which is exactly what an all-in denominator is supposed to expose. That is the useful reading of this result: not "the ASIC is 2× better than the GPU", but "a 700 W part fed by HBM4 turns about half of its raw bandwidth-per-watt edge into delivered tokens once the whole rack is on the meter".

The HBM generation is the elephant. Blackwell carries HBM3e; Nvidia's HBM4 part is Rubin, at roughly 22 TB/s per GPU and a 900–1 150 W compute die — the same bandwidth-per-watt class as Jalapeño. SemiAnalysis reports Jalapeño still ahead of Rubin on tokens per megawatt in their runs, but the gap is a fraction of the Blackwell gap. Rubin is the like-for-like comparison, and Rubin is shipping to customers now while Jalapeño is at engineering-sample stage.

TimelineThe calendar

Tape-out November 2025. Engineering samples August 2026. First deployments in OpenAI's own fleet at very small volume by the end of 2026, the bulk of the ramp at the end of 2027. The silicon that was benchmarked is an early stepping; OpenAI reportedly expects the B0 revision to add about 25 % in performance per watt. By the time Jalapeño runs at volume, the shipping Nvidia reference will not be Blackwell, and OpenAI's own hardware lead said as much when the numbers went out.

MethodHow I read a vendor-first benchmark

The same checklist I apply to my own measurements on the Mandelbrot case study applies here — only the scale of the machine differs.

✓

Find the denominator. Per-chip watts and all-in megawatts differ by 2× or more. A perf/W headline without the denominator is not a number.

✓

Normalize by bytes per second per watt first. For decode, memory bandwidth per watt is the roofline. Credit architecture only with what is left after that.

✓

Locate the operating point. One point on an interactivity-vs-throughput curve is a marketing number; the curve is the engineering number.

✓

List what was disabled. Here speculative decoding was off on the vendor's own chip — a handicap in the right direction. It is often the other way around.

✓

Compare shipping silicon with shipping silicon. An engineering sample vs a two-year-old memory generation is a preview, not a benchmark.

What survives scrutiny: a 700 W ASIC with six HBM4 stacks delivers about 1.9× Blackwell's tokens per kilowatt on GPT-OSS 120B at 8k/1k, with speculative decoding off. What does not: the idea that this says much about architecture beyond "newer memory, leaner host, one die tuned for one job". Both are worth knowing — the second is why the cost of a token keeps falling.

Why this matters to me beyond the spec sheet: every agent I build or consult on is priced in tokens, and tokens are priced in watts. When the lab that runs the model also designs the silicon for it, the floor under inference cost keeps moving down — and the model-routing and cost-control work that already pays for itself today pays more tomorrow.
